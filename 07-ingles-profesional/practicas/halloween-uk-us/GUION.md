# Guion de ensayo — Two stops, one spooky night

**Rubén: 00:00–03:00, diapositivas 1–5. Oliver: 03:00–06:00, diapositivas 6–10.**

Texto para preparar, no para leer delante de la clase. Las diapositivas solo muestran pistas. Las acotaciones en castellano no se pronuncian. El público imaginado son niños de unos 8–11 años; vocabulario sencillo con algunas expresiones del curso. Los tiempos son un objetivo de ensayo, no una duración medida: cronometradlo en voz alta y ajustaremos el texto si hace falta.

El anexo de fuentes es la diapositiva 11 y no se expone durante los seis minutos. Inglaterra es el foco de Rubén dentro de una comparación UK/US; las raíces compartidas se explican antes. Las escenas de los personajes son inventadas para practicar gramática, no costumbres documentadas.

## Rubén — 00:00 a 03:00

### 1. Two stops. One spooky night. — 00:00–00:25 (25 s)

**Pistas:** hello → Halloween → two stops → England first.

> Hello, everyone! I’m Rubén, and this is Oliver. Today we’re taking you on a little Halloween adventure. Halloween is on the thirty-first of October. Our first stop is England, in the United Kingdom. Then Oliver takes us to the United States. Are you ready? Let’s go!

**Acción:** saludar y señalar las dos casas. Dejar un segundo para un «yes»; no esperar una conversación.

### 2. A very old story. — 00:25–01:15 (50 s)

**Pistas:** ancient → Samhain → Ireland/Scotland → winter → Christian traditions.

> Our story has ancient roots. Ancient means very, very old! One root is Samhain, an old Celtic festival linked to Ireland and Scotland. People celebrated the end of the harvest, before winter. They believed that spirits could visit our world. Spooky!
>
> Christian traditions also helped shape Halloween. The name comes from All Hallows’ Eve, the night before All Saints’ Day. England also had its own customs. England is one part of the UK. And Ireland is not all part of the UK. Halloween has more than one home!

**Acción:** señalar las dos raíces. Pronunciar Samhain aproximadamente **/ˈsaʊ.ɪn/** («SOW-in», primera parte como *cow*). No dar fechas medievales que no necesitamos.

### 3. Pagan? Religious? — 01:15–01:50 (35 s)

**Pistas:** pagan roots → Christian links → today → families.

> Is Halloween pagan or religious? Samhain was a pagan festival: it was not Christian. All Saints’ Day is a Christian celebration on the first of November. Both are part of the story.
>
> Today, for many families in England, Halloween is mostly about costumes, games and fun, not religion. Some celebrate, and some don’t. We can respect both choices.

**Acción:** recorrer con el dedo las tres etapas, sin leer cada etiqueta.

### 4. Little cakes. Big smiles. — 01:50–02:30 (40 s)

**Pistas:** soul cakes → prayers → today → sweets → apples.

> In the past, some people in England went from door to door and received little soul cakes in return for prayers for the dead. Prayers are words people say to God.
>
> Today, some children wear costumes and go trick-or-treating for sweets. Families also carve pumpkins or play games. In apple bobbing, you catch an apple with your teeth, without your hands! But these customs are not only English, and families celebrate differently.

**Acción:** señalar el pastel y la manzana. Hacer un pequeño gesto con las manos detrás de la espalda, sin montar una demostración real con agua.

### 5. Meet our friendly ghost. — 02:30–03:00 (30 s)

**Pistas:** imaginary → is wearing → has decorated → has been decorating → Oliver.

> Look at our imaginary ghost! It’s friendly, not scary. It is wearing a purple hat. It has already decorated three pumpkins. It has been decorating for two hours! That’s a lot of work for one little ghost.
>
> Do you like its hat? I think it looks wonderful! Now our ghost is ready to travel. Oliver, let’s cross the Atlantic!

**Acción:** señalar sombrero, tres calabazas y reloj. Pregunta breve con un gesto; pasar a Oliver justo en torno al minuto tres.

## Oliver — 03:00 a 06:00

### 6. Old stories. A new home. — 03:00–03:40 (40 s)

**Pistas:** immigrants → 1800s → turnips → pumpkins → has changed.

> Thanks, Rubén! Now we’re in the United States. In the eighteen hundreds, Irish and Scottish immigrants brought Halloween customs with them. Immigrants are people who move to live in another country.
>
> People carved lanterns from turnips, a root vegetable. In America, people started using pumpkins instead. A pumpkin lantern is called a jack-o’-lantern. Halloween has changed over time. Old ideas have found new homes!

**Acción:** señalar el nabo y la calabaza. *Eighteen hundreds* = siglo XIX; no decir que Halloween nació en Estados Unidos.

### 7. Knock, knock. — 03:40–04:20 (40 s)

**Pistas:** costumes → knock → candy → polite → generous.

> Many children dress up and go trick-or-treating. They usually visit houses in the evening with an adult. They knock and say, “Trick or treat!” Then a neighbour gives them candy.
>
> In Britain, people usually say “sweets”. In America, they usually say “candy”. Same tasty idea! A generous neighbour shares treats. Generous means happy to give. What do polite children say? “Thank you!” Let’s practise: “Trick or treat!” … “Thank you!”

**Acción:** dos golpecitos suaves en el aire. Dar solo unos segundos para repetir; si nadie responde, decir tú las frases y continuar. Ir con un adulto es una recomendación de seguridad, no una regla que cumplan todas las familias.

### 8. Big pumpkin. Tiny superhero. — 04:20–05:00 (40 s)

**Pistas:** imaginary → is wearing → enormous/tiny → already → for hours.

> Look at this imaginary scene! This child is wearing a red cape. The pumpkin is enormous, and our superhero looks tiny next to it. Enormous means very big. Tiny means very small.
>
> The child has already collected some candy. The family has been making the costume for hours. They’re very creative! Costumes can be ghosts, witches or superheroes. Some costumes are absolutely hilarious! That means very, very funny. Do you prefer spooky or funny costumes?

**Acción:** mostrar grande/pequeño con las manos. La escena tiene una historia inventada: su familia lleva horas preparando el disfraz y el niño ya ha recogido caramelos. No afirmar que esto sea una foto o un dato real.

### 9. Different words. Lots in common. — 05:00–05:35 (35 s)

**Pistas:** sweets/candy → shared customs → often bigger → not everybody.

> What is different? England has older customs such as soul cakes. In the US, pumpkin lanterns became very popular, and celebrations are often bigger. But costumes, pumpkins and trick-or-treating exist in both places today. Superheroes are not only American!
>
> Halloween has pagan roots and Christian links. Today, many American families enjoy it as a non-religious celebration, just like many families in England. Every family is different.

**Acción:** comparar las dos columnas y luego señalar «BOTH». No convertir diferencias de frecuencia o vocabulario en reglas absolutas.

### 10. A ghost or a superhero? — 05:35–06:00 (25 s)

**Pistas:** choose → ghost/superhero → same date → thank you.

> One last question! What would you wear: a ghost costume or a superhero costume? Hands up for a ghost! Now hands up for a superhero!
>
> Both are great choices. We have visited two places with different words and shared traditions. Same date, lots of imagination! Thank you for joining our little Halloween adventure. Happy Halloween!

**Acción:** dos votaciones rápidas levantando la mano. Cerrar aquí, sin avanzar automáticamente a las fuentes.

## Cómo ensayarlo sin leer

1. Leer una vez para entender. Consultar `GUIA-LINGUISTICA.md` si una palabra o estructura cuesta.
2. Ensayar cada diapositiva mirando solo sus pistas. Es mejor decir la idea con otras palabras que recitar un párrafo.
3. Cronometrar por separado. El objetivo inicial es hablar a unas **110–120 palabras/minuto**, con pausas y unos segundos de participación. La velocidad real de cada persona manda.
4. Ensayar juntos el relevo. Rubén termina su diapositiva 5; Oliver avanza a la 6.
5. Si os pasáis, quitar primero explicaciones repetidas, no los cuatro apartados del enunciado ni los ejemplos de tiempos verbales. Si vais cortos, señalar y explicar la ilustración; no añadir vocabulario difícil de improviso.

### Salvavidas si os quedáis en blanco

| Pantalla | Arranque corto |
|---|---|
| Orígenes | *It has very old roots.* |
| Religión | *It has pagan roots and Christian links.* |
| Inglaterra | *In England, some children…* |
| Imagen | *Look! It is wearing…* / *The child is wearing…* |
| USA | *Now let’s look at the United States.* |
| Comparación | *In both places, people…* |

No memorizar una pronunciación española del texto entero. Escuchar un diccionario para las palabras nuevas y practicar frases cortas. No hay grabaciones de audio incluidas.
